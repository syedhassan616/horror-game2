# 01 — Vision, Pillars & Competitive Position

---

## 1.1 The Elevator Pitch

> **SECONDHEART** is a 2D pixel-art RPG about the people you carry — where your
> companions aren't party members, they're *your second body in combat*, tied to you
> by an elastic tether you drag through bullet-hell. The world is being quietly
> stripped of its capacity to love, by a machine that thinks it's doing everyone a
> favour. It might be.

**Hook order for a 90-second trailer:**
1. (0:00) A woman looks at a photograph of her daughter and says, flatly: *"I know that I loved her."*
2. (0:12) Two glyphs. One tether. A wall of light. The tether sweeps through it and the screen fills with gold.
3. (0:34) A companion is cut away mid-fight. The tether goes slack. The player's dodge ability is *gone* and the HUD says so.
4. (0:52) The Assayer, gentle: *"Grief is the interest on love. I am forgiving the debt."*
5. (1:10) Title. *SECONDHEART.*

---

## 1.2 Statement of Intent

Undertale's achievement was not its mechanics; it was that it made the player
*responsible*. Its systems taught you that the game was watching, and then it made
you live with what it saw. Cloning its mechanics reproduces none of that.

SECONDHEART goes after the same organ by a different route. Where Undertale asked
*"will you kill?"*, SECONDHEART asks a question with no clean answer:

> **If you could stop hurting over someone you lost — permanently, painlessly, right
> now — would you? And do you have the right to decide that for anyone else?**

The game never answers. It builds a world where reasonable, decent, funny people have
landed on every side of that question, gives the player mechanical power to enforce
their view, and then shows them the town six hours later.

## 1.3 The Four Pillars, Expanded

### Pillar I — *The people you carry are the reason you survive.*

**Mechanical expression:** Combat is two-body. Your companion is not an ally who acts
on their turn; they are the far end of your dodge. Each companion supplies a distinct
tether physic and a distinct movement verb.

**Emotional expression:** When a companion is severed, injured, or leaves, the player
loses a *capability they had built muscle memory around*. Grief is delivered as motor
confusion. The dodge you've done a thousand times no longer works.

**Test:** A player who loses Tilly in Act II should, within ten seconds of the next
fight, instinctively press PLANT, get nothing, and die. That death is the scene.

### Pillar II — *Peace is harder than violence, and worth it.*

**Mechanical expression:** SEVER is one button and always works. UNKNOT requires
(a) INSIGHT, earned only by deliberately dragging your tether *through* live fire, and
(b) a correct diagnosis chosen from evidence you had to actually read.

**Anti-pattern to avoid:** Undertale's ACT-then-SPARE loop can degrade to menu
spamming. UNKNOT never accepts a repeated wrong answer — wrong diagnoses cost INSIGHT
and escalate the enemy's phase.

**Test:** In a blind playtest, the pacifist clear of a mid-game boss should take
**longer and require more inputs-per-minute** than the violent clear, and players
should describe it as "the hard way" without being told to.

### Pillar III — *Grief is not a villain.*

**Mechanical expression:** There is no evil alignment, no red-eyed sprite, no
"corruption" bar. The eight hidden values include no axis on which "good" is a
direction (see §06).

**Narrative expression:** Every antagonist is load-bearing correct about something.
Sister Annike, who severs willing people at Hushfell, is running the most humane
hospice in Vesselmere. The player should be unable to argue her down without
conceding a point.

**Test:** Post-playthrough survey: *"Was the Assayer wrong?"* We are aiming for a
50/40/10 split (no / yes / "I don't know and it's been bothering me"). A 90% "yes" is
a failure of writing.

### Pillar IV — *Every choice leaves a scar you can walk past.*

**Mechanical expression:** Consequences are written into level state, NPC schedules,
shop inventories, the world map, and the music stems — before they are written into
dialogue. If you got someone severed, their house is still there, and they're in it,
polite and empty.

**Test:** Take two save files at the same story beat from different routes, walk a
QA tester through Wyndmarrow in both with dialogue disabled, and ask what happened.
They should be able to tell you.

## 1.4 Player Fantasy

The player is **not** a hero, a chosen one, or a warrior. The player is a **carrier** —
the last person in Vesselmere with room in their chest. The fantasy is:

- *"I am the one who remembers for everyone else."*
- *"I keep these people alive by keeping them close, literally, in a fight."*
- *"I can put back what was taken — but only if I'm willing to hand back the pain with it."*

The fantasy is **custodial**, not martial. Power fantasy beats are replaced with
**stewardship beats**: the moment a severed NPC recognises a smell and starts crying
without knowing why is the game's equivalent of a critical hit.

## 1.5 Tone Contract

SECONDHEART is funny. This is not decoration — it is load-bearing structural support
for the sad parts, and it is the primary defence against the game becoming a misery
simulator.

**The tone rules:**

1. **Humour comes from character, never from the narrator.** No winking, no
   lampshading, no "haha, video games."
2. **The funniest character in any region is the one carrying the heaviest thing.**
   Barro the Loomworks foreman does a five-minute bit about regulation eyelets. Barro
   is illegally hand-weaving a Ward for his severed husband and will be executed for it.
3. **Never undercut a landing.** Once a scene turns, it stays turned. Comedy sets up
   grief; grief is never rescued by comedy in the same scene.
4. **Small absurdities, played straight.** Vesselmere has a bureaucracy of feeling.
   There is a form for grief (Form 12-B, *Petition of Continued Regard*). It has a
   queue. The queue is very long. Nobody in-world finds this funny.
5. **No character is the joke.** Comic characters get the same interiority as tragic
   ones. If a player laughs at an NPC in Act I, that NPC gets a scene in Act III that
   makes the laugh retroactively uncomfortable.

**Tonal reference points** (feel, not content): the domestic comedy of *Fishing for
Catfish* in a hospice waiting room; Terry Pratchett's insistence that bureaucracy is
both hilarious and how atrocities actually happen; the specific ache of finding a
loved one's handwriting on a shopping list.

## 1.6 Target Audience & Positioning

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Primary** | 18–34, played *Undertale / Hades / Celeste / Disco Elysium*, wants a story-forward RPG with real mechanical teeth |
| **Secondary** | Bullet-hell / action players who bounce off text-heavy RPGs — hooked by the tether's skill ceiling |
| **Tertiary** | Narrative-only players — served by the full accessibility path (§13.6) which preserves 100% of story and all endings |
| **Rating target** | ESRB **T** / PEGI **12** — thematic (grief, assisted memory-loss, one on-screen death), no gore, no sexual content |
| **Platforms** | PC (Win/Mac/Linux) at launch; Switch/Steam Deck verified as design constraints from day one |
| **Price point** | $19.99 USD |
| **Comparable titles** | *Undertale*, *OMORI*, *In Stars and Time*, *Hades*, *Chicory* |

**Steam Deck / Switch as a design constraint:** the tether must be fully playable on a
single analogue stick + two shoulder buttons. If a tether verb cannot be bound
comfortably to a Deck, the verb is wrong. This constraint is checked at every combat
design review, not at port time.

## 1.7 What "Indie Game of the Year" Requires — Our Checklist

We are explicit about what actually wins this award, and where each is delivered:

| Requirement | Our answer | Doc |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A mechanic nobody has seen | The Tether — two-body dodging where the *connection* is the hitbox that earns peace | §05 |
| A moment people post clips of | Act II's *Slack Tether* — the game removes a companion mid-fight and does not pause | §02, §08 |
| A line people tattoo on themselves | *"I know that I loved her. I can't find where I kept it."* | §02 |
| A boss people replay for fun | **Vellum**, the Assayer's clerk — fought four times, becomes progressively worse at his job as he starts to care | §08 |
| A secret that takes the community weeks | **The First Ward** in the Keeping — gated behind a nine-region cross-referencing puzzle | §08.6 |
| A soundtrack that charts | 34 tracks, four leitmotifs, choice-reactive stems | §11 |
| Replay value that isn't a chore | Three structurally different routes; Act III is a *different set of regions* per route | §10 |
| Accessibility as craft, not compliance | Full-story path with dodging automated; every ending reachable | §13.6 |

## 1.8 Scope Reality Check

| Metric | Target | Rationale |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main story | 4h30m – 6h | Tight enough to be replayed 3×, long enough to earn Act III |
| Completionist | 8–10h | Superboss chain + 35 side quests + collectibles |
| Full 3-route mastery | ~22h | Routes share ~35% of content by scene count |
| Regions | 14 | 10 core + 2 route-exclusive + 2 secret |
| Major characters | 12 | Each with full arc, theme, battle mechanic |
| Named NPCs | 46 | Each with memory state and ≥3 conversation stages |
| Enemy types | 84 | Across 9 families, each family sharing an AI trunk |
| Bosses | 10 major / 5 optional / 2 secret | |
| Side quests | 35 | Zero fetch quests. 9 are *failable*. |
| Endings | 5 | True / Bad / Neutral / Secret / Hidden |
| Achievements | 78 | |
| Music tracks | 34 | |
| Team size assumed | 6–8 FTE | 18-month schedule, §17 |

**The scope we deliberately refused:** open world, crafting, romance options, an
overworld encounter grind, procedural anything, and voice acting. Each was evaluated
and cut in service of Pillar IV — a hand-authored world is the only kind whose scars
you can walk past.
